About this website

Some NorSand concepts such as the state parameter, image point, internal cap, limiting yield surface, dilatancy, and yield-surface evolution can be difficult to understand from equations and static diagrams alone. Here, these concepts are explained gradually and connected to interactive visualizations of conventional triaxial testsA laboratory test in which a soil specimen, usually cylindrical, is loaded under controlled stress and drainage conditions. The purpose of this website is to make these ideas easier to learn.

The website is organized around three goals:

  • Learn the basic ideas of Critical State Soil Mechanics and NorSand.
  • Visualize how stress, volume, state, dilatancy, and the NorSand yield surface evolve during loading.
  • Apply the model through validated examples, parameter sets, and, in later stages, reproducible implementation and calibration resources.

Understanding NorSand

Soils may look simple, but their behavior can change dramatically depending on how densely the particles are packed, their current effective stress Stress carried by the soil skeleton. For saturated soil, it is total stress minus pore-water pressure, whether water is allowed to drain, and how they are loaded. A relatively dense sand may develop a dilative response and a peak strength, whereas a relatively loose sand generally tends to contract. Under undrained loading, this contractive tendency can generate positive excess pore-water pressurePressure of water in the soil pores. Changes in pore-water pressure directly affect effective stress, reduce effective stress, and, in sufficiently loose states, lead to strain-softeningReduction in deviator stress or mobilized strength with continued strain after a peak or static liquefactionUndrained loss of strength in a loose, contractive soil as pore-water pressure builds up and effective stress decreases. The same soil can therefore respond very differently under different initial density and loading conditions. Critical State Soil Mechanics, commonly abbreviated as CSSM, provides a framework for understanding these different responses. Its central idea is that a given soil has a characteristic critical-state relationship. Samples of that soil may start from different densities and effective stress levels and may follow different stress–strain paths, but continued monotonic shearing tends to move their states toward the critical state defined for that material. At the critical state, the soil continues to shear without further change in mean effective stress, deviator stressShear-related stress measure, q, or volume.

NorSand is a constitutive model developed by Jefferies (1993) for sands and other particulate materials within the CSSM framework. A constitutive model is a set of mathematical relationships used to predict how a material responds to loading. In geotechnical engineering, these relationships connect changes in stress to changes in strain, volume, strength, and pore-water pressure. Rather than treating loose and dense soils as fundamentally different materials, NorSand represents both within the same critical-state framework. Their different responses arise mainly from the soil's current density, effective stress, and stress-stateThe current combination of stresses acting on the soil, commonly described here by p′ and q position relative to its critical state line. Using this information, NorSand calculates how stress, strain, volume change, dilatancy, and strength evolve during loading. NorSand has become particularly important in mine-tailings engineering because it can represent both dilative behavior and strongly contractive behavior associated with static liquefaction. However, the ideas behind NorSand are useful well beyond tailings. They provide a general way to understand how granular soils evolve from their initial condition toward critical state.

Stress variables used in triaxial loading

This website initially focuses on conventional axisymmetric triaxial compression. In this test, the specimen is subjected to a major principal effective stress, σ1′, in the axial direction and two equal minor principal effective stresses, σ2′=σ3′, in the radial directions. The mean effective stress is:

p′=σ1′+2σ3′3

and the deviator stress is

q=σ1′−σ3′

Their ratio is called the stress ratio:

η=qp′

The stress ratio provides a convenient measure of how strongly the specimen is being sheared relative to the pressure confining it. The soil does not respond directly to total stress alone. Its strength and deformation are governed mainly by effective stress, the stress carried through the soil skeleton after accounting for pore-water pressure. This distinction becomes especially important in undrained loading. When water cannot escape, pore-water pressure may increase, causing the effective stress to decrease even though the externally applied total stress continues to increase. In tensor formA compact way to write stress relationships using tensor notation, the effective-stress principle may be written as

σ′=σ−uI

where σ is the total stress tensorA mathematical representation of the total stress on a soil element in all directions at a point, before subtracting pore-water pressure, u is pore-water pressure, and I is the identity tensor.

Critical-state idea

NorSand is built within the framework of Critical State Soil Mechanics, commonly abbreviated as CSSM. The main idea of CSSM is that soil strength cannot be described by friction alone. Soil behavior also depends on its density, effective stress, and position relative to critical state. When a soil is sheared through sufficiently large strain, it may eventually reach a condition in which it continues deforming without further change in stress or volume. This condition is called the critical state.Critical State Soil Mechanics was developed largely through early research on clays. Its application to sands was subsequently advanced through several developments, including the state parameter introduced by Been and Jefferies (1985). The state parameter later became a central component of the NorSand model developed by Jefferies (1993).

At critical state:

  • the mean effective stress remains constant;
  • the deviator stress remains constant;
  • the void ratioRatio of void volume to solid volume, e=Vv⁄Vs remains constant;
  • the soil continues to accumulate shear strain.

In triaxial stress variables, the critical state is represented by

q=Mp′

where q is the deviator stress, p′ is the mean effective stress, and M is the critical-state stress ratio. The critical state can be thought of as the long-term destination of the soil during monotonic shearing. Different specimens may begin from different densities and stresses and may follow very different stress–strain paths, but continued shearing tends to bring them toward critical state. This does not mean that the initial state or loading history is unimportant. These factors strongly influence whether the soil contracts or dilates, whether it reaches a peak strength, how much pore-water pressure develops, and how much strain is required before critical state is approached.

Void ratio and soil packing

The packing of the soil is described using the void ratio,

e=VvVs

where Vv is the volume of voids and Vs is the volume of solids. A higher void ratio generally means that the particles are packed more loosely. A lower void ratio generally means that they are packed more densely. However, void ratio alone is not enough to predict soil behavior. The same void ratio can indicate a relatively dense state at one effective stress and a relatively loose state at another because the critical-state void ratio changes with mean effective stress. The density of the soil must therefore be interpreted together with its current effective stress. This is one of the central ideas behind the state parameter used by NorSand.

Critical state line

The critical state does not correspond to one single stress or one single void ratio. Instead, there is a range of critical states associated with different effective stress levels. These conditions form the critical state line, or CSL. The CSL is commonly shown in two spaces.

CSL in p′−q space: In triaxial compression, the critical state line is q=Mp′. It passes through the origin with slope equal to the critical-state stress ratio, M.

CSL in void ratio–mean effective stress space: The CSL also describes how the critical-state void ratio decreases as mean effective stress increases. A commonly used semi-logarithmic form is:

ec=Γ−λ10log⁡10(p′pref)

where ec is the critical-state void ratio at the current mean effective stress, Γ is the critical-state void ratio at the reference pressure pref, λ10 controls the slope of the CSL in e−log⁡10p′ space, and pref is the reference pressure used to make the logarithm dimensionless. Some formulations use the natural logarithm instead of the base-10 logarithm. In that case, the slope parameters are related by:

λ=λ10ln10

The implementation associated with this website allows the CSL to be represented in two ways: Semi-logarithmic CSL and

Curved CSL

ec=a−b(p′pref)c

The curved form can be useful when a straight line in semi-logarithmic space does not adequately represent the measured critical states over the relevant stress range. The CSL is treated as a material-specific reference relationship for the soil and stress range considered. Changing the initial density or stress state changes the specimen's position relative to the CSL, not the CSL itself. In practice, the CSL should be established from multiple tests covering a suitable range of initial densities and mean effective stresses. These may include drained and undrained tests, provided the specimens are sheared sufficiently for reliable critical-state conditions to be identified.

State parameter

The state parameter is one of the most important concepts in NorSand. It was introduced by Been and Jefferies (1985) and is defined as

ψ=e−ec(p′)

It measures the vertical distance between the current state of the soil and the critical state line at the same mean effective stress. When ψ>0, the current void ratio is greater than the critical void ratio. The soil is looser than the critical state at the same effective stress; such a state generally has a stronger tendency to contract during shearing. When ψ<0 the current void ratio is smaller than the critical void ratio. The soil is denser than the critical state at the same effective stress; such a state generally has a stronger tendency to dilate during shearing. At critical state, ψ=0. The state parameter is useful because it combines density and effective stress in one quantity. Two specimens with the same void ratio may behave differently because they are subjected to different effective stress levels — their state parameters reveal this difference. For an initial mean effective stress p0′, the initial state is defined by

ψ0=e0−ec(p0′)

or equivalently,

e0=ec(p0′)+ψ0.

NorSand yield surface

The yield surface defines the boundary of the elastic domain in stress space. Stress changes that remain inside the yield surface are treated as elastic and recoverable. When the stress state lies on the yield surface, continued loading that would otherwise move it outside the surface produces plastic deformation. In NorSand, the outer yield surface is expressed in terms of p′, q, an image pressure pi′, and an image stress ratio Mi. In triaxial stress space, it is written as:

qp′=Mi[1−ln⁡(p′pi′)]

The image pressure pi′ controls the size of the yield surface. During plastic loading, the current stress state lies on the yield surface, while the surface evolves as pi′ and Mi change during shearing.

Image point

The image point is located at the apex of the NorSand yield surface. At this point p′=pi′, η=Mi and Dp=0. The image pressure pi′ controls the size of the current yield surface, so a larger pi′ corresponds to a larger yield surface for a given Mi. The image point does not generally coincide with the current stress state. Instead, it is an internal reference condition used by NorSand to relate the current soil state, stress–dilatancy response, and evolution of the yield surface. As loading proceeds, pi′ evolves, causing the yield surface to expand or contract.

Contraction and dilation

When soil is sheared, its particles rearrange. A loose arrangement may allow particles to move into the available void spaces, producing contraction. A dense arrangement may require particles to climb over one another, producing dilation. NorSand describes the plastic volume-change tendency using plastic dilatancy,

Dp=dεvpdεqp

In the implementation used here, plastic dilatancy is expressed as

Dp=Mi−η

where Mi is the stress ratio at the image point and η=q⁄p′ is the current stress ratio. With the compression-positive convention used here, η<Mi⇒Dp>0 represents plastic contraction, η=Mi⇒Dp=0, represents zero plastic dilatancy, and η>Mi⇒Dp<0, represents plastic dilation. A soil described as dense does not necessarily dilate from the very beginning of loading. Dense specimens may initially contract, reach a transformation point at which Dp=0, and then begin to dilate. Similarly, contraction and strain-softening should not be treated as identical concepts. The response depends on the evolving stress ratio, state parameter, yield surface, and loading path.

Limiting dilatancy and the internal cap

Without a limiting condition, NorSand could predict unrealistically large dilation for states that are denser than critical state. NorSand therefore imposes a limiting-dilatancy condition linked to the soil state. In triaxial compression, the state–dilatancy parameter χtc, together with the state parameter evaluated at the image condition, determines the limiting image pressure pi,max⁡′. This limits the maximum size to which the outer NorSand yield surface can expand for the current soil state. This limiting condition is commonly described as an internal cap because it restricts the admissible extent of the outer yield surface. It is not an additional external failure envelope. In the present visualizations, users can observe the current stress state, image point, limiting image condition, and the evolution of the current and limiting image pressures with axial strain.

Hardening and softening

The NorSand hardening law controls the evolution of the image pressure pi′. When pi′<pi,max⁡′, plastic shearing generally causes the image pressure to increase and the yield surface to expand — this is referred to as hardening. When pi′>pi,max⁡′, the hardening law drives the image pressure downward, causing the yield surface to contract — this is referred to as softening. The model's hardening parameters control how pi′ evolves with plastic straining. At critical state, the state parameter and plastic dilatancy approach zero, and continued shearing occurs without further change in mean effective stress, deviator stress, or void ratio.

Elastic stiffness

Before and during plastic loading, NorSand requires an elastic relationship. The formulation used for the simulations presented on this website adopts a pressure-dependent elastic shear modulusElastic stiffness controlling recoverable shear deformation:

G=Gref(p′pref)m

where Gref is the shear modulus at the reference pressure, G is the current shear modulus, m controls how stiffness changes with effective stress. For example, m=1 gives Gp′=constant, while m=0.5 gives G∝p′. A rigidity index may also be written as:

Ir=Gp′

It remains constant when m=1, but varies with mean effective stress for other values of m. The elastic parameters mainly influence the initial stiffness and the elastic part of the response, while the critical-state, dilatancy, and hardening parameters govern the subsequent elastoplastic response. Poisson's ratioElastic parameter that relates lateral strain to axial strain during elastic deformation, ν, or an equivalent bulk-modulus specification, is also required so that the elastic shear and volumetric responses are calculated consistently. The elastic bulk modulusElastic stiffness controlling recoverable volume change due to changes in mean stress is:

K=2G(1+ν)3(1−2ν)

Drained and undrained loading

This website initially presents four conventional triaxial compression cases:

  • dense drained;
  • loose drained;
  • dense undrained;
  • loose undrained;

Drained Loading

In a drained test, pore water is allowed to enter or leave the specimen and excess pore-water pressure is not sustained. The specimen can therefore change volume during shearing. A specimen that is denser than critical state may contract initially and then dilate, often developing a peak deviator stress before softening toward critical state. A specimen that is looser than critical state generally remains contractive and commonly hardens toward critical state without developing a pronounced peak above the critical-state strength. For the idealized drained simulations presented here, excess pore-water pressure is taken as, Δu=0.

Undrained Loading

In an undrained test, water is not allowed to drain from the specimen. For a saturated soil, the total volume remains approximately constant. However, the soil skeleton may still have a tendency to contract or dilate. A contractive tendency produces positive excess pore-water pressure and reduces mean effective stress. A dilative tendency potentially produces negative excess pore-water pressure and increases mean effective stress. For conventional triaxial compression at constant total confining pressure, excess pore-water pressure is calculated as:

Δu=q−q03−(p′−p0′)

For an initially isotropic condition with q0=0,

Δu=p0′+q3−p′

The website visualizations include a separate plot of excess pore-water pressure against axial strain for undrained simulations. NorSand parameters are generally calibrated together using several triaxial tests rather than fitting each parameter independently from a single response curve. A reliable calibration should reproduce the stress–strain response, the volumetric or pore-pressure response, and the effects of initial state and confining pressure.

NorSand parameters

Implementing NorSand requires a set of material parameters that describe the soil’s critical-state line, strength, plastic response, limiting dilatancy, and elastic stiffness. Many of these parameters correspond directly to the concepts introduced earlier. The CSL parameters define the position and shape of the critical-state line. The strength and plasticity parameters control the stress ratio, dilatancy, and evolution of the yield surface. The elastic parameters control the recoverable part of the response and the initial stiffness.

These parameters are not usually calibrated one at a time from a single test. Instead, they are determined together from a set of drained and undrained triaxial tests covering different initial densities and confining stresses. A reliable parameter set should reproduce the CSL, stress-strain response, volumetric response, and pore-pressure response consistently across the available tests.

The table below summarizes the main NorSand parameters and their roles. The specific values used in the interactive simulations are provided in the following Simulation Parameters section.

Interactive triaxial simulations

Each interactive simulation follows the response of a triaxial specimen as axial strain increases. The strain slider at the bottom allows the user to move through the test and observe how the stress state, volume change, and internal NorSand variables evolve together. The large panel on the left shows the stress path in p′−q space. The black curve represents the loading path, the red marker shows the current stress state, the purple curve shows the current NorSand yield surface, and the green marker shows the image point. The blue dashed line is the critical state line. As the slider is moved, this panel shows how the current stress state moves relative to the yield surface, image point, and critical state line.

The plots on the right show the corresponding response at the same axial strain. The top plot shows deviator stress versus axial strain, which indicates whether the specimen is hardening, reaching a peak, or softening. The middle plot shows the volume-change response for drained simulations, or the excess pore-water pressure response for undrained simulations. This helps identify contraction, dilation, and pore-pressure generation. The bottom plot shows the current void ratio relative to the critical state line in e−log⁡p′ space. This helps explain whether the specimen is looser or denser than critical state during loading.

These plots connect the observable triaxial response to the internal NorSand mechanisms. The objective is to show why that response occurs through the evolution of the stress path, yield surface, image point, dilatancy, hardening or softening, and movement toward critical state.

Drained dense specimen

With an initial state parameter of ψ0=−0.158, this specimen starts denser than the critical state. It shows only a small amount of initial contraction, reaching a maximum volumetric strain of about 0.4% near ε1=1.3%. After this point, the plastic dilatancy Dp becomes negative, and the specimen begins to dilate continuously, reaching approximately -5.7% volumetric strain by ε1≈19%. The deviator stress increases rapidly to a peak of about 1656 kPa near ε1=5%, followed by softening toward critical state. This softening occurs as the image pressure pi′ exceeds its limiting value pi,max⁡′ and is gradually reduced. By the end of the simulation, the stress ratio q⁄p′ has decreased to about 1.52, which is still slightly above M=1.45. This indicates that the specimen is approaching critical state but has not fully reached it within the simulated strain range.

Drained loose specimen

With an initial state parameter of ψ0=0.127, this specimen starts looser than the critical state. The volumetric response remains contractive throughout the simulation, and the specimen does not dilate. By the end of the simulation, the volumetric strain is still increasing and reaches nearly 8%. The deviator stress increases steadily without forming a peak and is still rising at ε1≈23%. This occurs because the image pressure pi,⁡′ remains below the limiting image pressure pi,max⁡′ throughout the simulation. Therefore, the response is governed by continued hardening rather than softening. The final stress ratio q⁄p′≈1.38 remains below M=1.45, showing that the specimen is still hardening toward critical state rather than having reached it.

The next two visualizations show undrained triaxial compression responses for dense and loose specimens. The dense undrained case, with ψ0=−0.032, and the loose undrained case, with ψ0=0.076, illustrate two contrasting undrained responses.

Undrained dense specimen

In the dense case, the specimen initially tends to contract, which generates positive excess pore-water pressure. The excess pore-water pressure rises to about 226 kPa within the first 2% axial strain, reducing the mean effective stress p′ to a minimum of ~304 kPa. As shearing continues, the response changes from contractive toward dilative. The excess pore-water pressure then decreases to about 68 kPa, while p′ recovers to nearly 628 kPa. This recovery in effective stress allows the deviator stress to continue increasing to about 909 kPa, ending almost exactly on the critical state line.

Undrained loose specimen

The loose case shows the opposite behavior. The deviator stress reaches only a small early peak of about 98 kPa near ε1=0.7%, before decreasing to 16 kPa by the end of the simulation. At the same time, excess pore-water pressure increases steadily to nearly 196 kPa, reducing the mean effective stress from 202 kPa to only about 11 kPa. This progressive loss of strength and effective stress under undrained loading represents static liquefaction in the model. The stress path moves toward very low p′ and q values near the origin.

Validation and current scope

The interactive simulations on this website are generated using a MATLAB material-point implementation of NorSand for conventional axisymmetric triaxial compression. The implementation was checked against four GeoStudio/Excel-VBA NorSand benchmark cases using the main response plots: q−ε1, p′−q, εv−ε1, and, for undrained loading, Δu−ε1.

The current version should be interpreted as a learning and research visualization tool for monotonic CID and CIU triaxial compression. It is not yet a general three-dimensional NorSand implementation. At this stage, the website does not cover arbitrary stress paths, principal-stress rotation, triaxial extension, plane-strain loading, cyclic loading, or general finite-element stress integration. These capabilities may be considered in future stages of the project.

Continue learning about NorSand

Readers new to NorSand may follow this learning path:

Effective stress → Critical state → Critical state line → State parameter → Dilatancy → Yield surface → Image point → Hardening and softening → Triaxial response

Readers can explore the material in different ways depending on their background. Those new to Critical State Soil Mechanics may begin with the introductory concepts and follow the suggested learning sequence, while readers already familiar with CSSM can proceed directly to the NorSand model components, validated triaxial examples, and interactive visualizations. The website is intended to connect NorSand theory with the response calculated during loading. Its explanations introduce the main concepts in accessible language, while the visualizations show how the stress state and internal variables evolve together. The objective is to help users understand not only the resulting stress–strain curves, but also the constitutive mechanisms that produce them. The material should be viewed as a guided learning resource rather than a complete derivation of NorSand. Readers seeking a rigorous treatment of the formulation, theoretical foundations, calibration, and broader applications should consult the original publications and the second edition of Soil Liquefaction: A Critical State Approach by Jefferies and Been.
