2. Core concepts

2.2 Critical-state idea

NorSand is built within the framework of Critical State Soil Mechanics, commonly abbreviated as CSSM. The main idea of CSSM is that soil strength cannot be described by friction alone. Soil behavior also depends on its density, effective stress, and position relative to critical state. When a soil is sheared through sufficiently large strain, it may eventually reach a condition in which it continues deforming without further change in stress or volume. This condition is called the critical state.Critical State Soil Mechanics was developed largely through early research on clays. Its application to sands was subsequently advanced through several developments, including the state parameter introduced by Been and Jefferies (1985). The state parameter later became a central component of the NorSand model developed by Jefferies (1993).

At critical state:

  • the mean effective stress remains constant;
  • the deviator stress remains constant;
  • the void ratioRatio of void volume to solid volume, e=Vv⁄Vs remains constant;
  • the soil continues to accumulate shear strain.

In triaxial stress variables, the critical state is represented by

q=Mp′

where q is the deviator stress, p′ is the mean effective stress, and M is the critical-state stress ratio. The critical state can be thought of as the long-term destination of the soil during monotonic shearing. Different specimens may begin from different densities and stresses and may follow very different stress–strain paths, but continued shearing tends to bring them toward critical state. This does not mean that the initial state or loading history is unimportant. These factors strongly influence whether the soil contracts or dilates, whether it reaches a peak strength, how much pore-water pressure develops, and how much strain is required before critical state is approached.
