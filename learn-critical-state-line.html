2. Core concepts

2.4 Critical state line

The critical state does not correspond to one single stress or one single void ratio. Instead, there is a range of critical states associated with different effective stress levels. These conditions form the critical state line, or CSL. The CSL is commonly shown in two spaces.

CSL in p′−q space: In triaxial compression, the critical state line is q=Mp′. It passes through the origin with slope equal to the critical-state stress ratio, M.

CSL in void ratio–mean effective stress space: The CSL also describes how the critical-state void ratio decreases as mean effective stress increases. A commonly used semi-logarithmic form is:

ec=Γ−λ10log⁡10(p′pref)

where ec is the critical-state void ratio at the current mean effective stress, Γ is the critical-state void ratio at the reference pressure pref, λ10 controls the slope of the CSL in e−log⁡10p′ space, and pref is the reference pressure used to make the logarithm dimensionless. Some formulations use the natural logarithm instead of the base-10 logarithm. In that case, the slope parameters are related by:

λ=λ10ln10

The implementation associated with this website allows the CSL to be represented in two ways: Semi-logarithmic CSL and

Curved CSL

ec=a−b(p′pref)c

The curved form can be useful when a straight line in semi-logarithmic space does not adequately represent the measured critical states over the relevant stress range. The CSL is treated as a material-specific reference relationship for the soil and stress range considered. Changing the initial density or stress state changes the specimen's position relative to the CSL, not the CSL itself. In practice, the CSL should be established from multiple tests covering a suitable range of initial densities and mean effective stresses. These may include drained and undrained tests, provided the specimens are sheared sufficiently for reliable critical-state conditions to be identified.
